The NCAA women’s tournament delivered another charged March moment on Sunday as Texas Tech Red Raiders forward Jalynn Bristow rose confidently from beyond the arc and drilled a three-pointer over LSU Tigers forward Grace Knox during the second half at the Maravich Assembly Center. In a game where every possession carried added weight, the shot stood out as a snapshot of poise under pressure.

Texas Tech entered the second round knowing it would need composure as much as talent against an LSU side playing on its home floor. The Red Raiders were tested by the Tigers’ energy, physicality, and crowd-fueled momentum, but Bristow’s willingness to take a difficult perimeter attempt reflected the fearlessness required to survive and advance in the national tournament.

The sequence itself captured the urgency of postseason basketball. Knox closed hard, extending the defense and forcing Bristow to shoot with little margin for error, yet the Texas Tech forward stayed balanced and decisive. Those are the moments that often define tournament games, when hesitation disappears and instinct takes over.

For LSU, the play was emblematic of the challenge of containing a confident opponent in the second half. Tournament basketball can hinge on a handful of possessions, and even strong defensive efforts sometimes end with a well-contested shot falling anyway. Knox’s pressure was there, but Bristow’s release and timing proved just a touch better.

The atmosphere in Baton Rouge added another layer to the contest. The Maravich Assembly Center provided the Tigers with a loud and emotional setting, the kind of environment that can rattle visiting teams. Instead, Texas Tech appeared intent on matching that intensity with execution, using timely shot-making to quiet the building and keep the game firmly in reach.

Bristow’s three-pointer also underscored the changing demands placed on modern forwards in the women’s game. Size alone is no longer enough in tournament play; versatility, spacing, and perimeter confidence are essential. By stepping out and converting from long range in traffic, Bristow showed the kind of multidimensional skill that can alter a defensive game plan.

As the second half unfolded, both programs were forced to balance urgency with discipline. LSU looked to impose itself through defensive pressure and interior strength, while Texas Tech searched for openings on the perimeter and moments of offensive clarity. Bristow’s basket served as one of those moments, offering a reminder that a single shot can change the emotional direction of a postseason matchup.

Whether remembered as a turning point or simply one of the afternoon’s standout plays, Bristow’s deep make over Knox fit perfectly into the larger drama of March. It was a flash of confidence, skill, and competitive nerve on one of college basketball’s biggest stages, the kind of image that endures long after the final buzzer sounds.
